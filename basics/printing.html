3D printing for electronics

Almost every project on this site ends up needing a printed part: a case, a mount, a bracket, a jig. And a printer is itself a machine that electronics can make better and safer. This is how the two fit together - and how to print a case that actually fits the board, first time.

Why these belong together

A breadboard project works on a desk. A finished project has to live somewhere - on a wall, in a car, in a greenhouse, on a shelf in the sun. The gap between those two is almost always an enclosure, and a printer is what closes it.

It runs the other way as well. A printer is a stepper-driven, heated, sensor-laden machine, and several projects here exist to make printing better - drying filament properly - or safer, by watching an unattended print for fire.

Choose the material by temperature, not by habit

This is the single most important decision in printing for electronics, and the one most often got wrong, because PLA prints so easily and looks perfect on the day.

PLA and anything warm

PLA starts to soften around 55 °C. That is below the air temperature near a working Jetson, inside a car on a summer day, or in a box on a sunny windowsill. It does not melt - it creeps, slowly, wherever a screw is pulling on it, and weeks later the case has warped and the lid no longer closes.

For anything that runs warm or lives somewhere warm, use PETG. It prints almost as easily and holds to about 80 °C. It is the right default for electronics enclosures.

Print a test plate before the case

A flat plate a few millimetres thick with nothing on it but the mounting posts, at the positions you measured. Print it, screw the board to it, and check every post lines up. It takes forty minutes and it is the difference between a case that fits and one you print three times.

Do it even for boards with a perfect published drawing. It also checks your printer. The Uno and Pico plates below are exactly this.

Designing around a real board

Tolerances: holes shrink, pegs grow

An FDM printer squashes each layer slightly as it lays it down, so every printed feature grows by a fraction of a millimetre. A modelled 3.2 mm hole prints nearer 3.0; a 3.2 mm peg prints nearer 3.4.

  • Holes for screws: add 0.2-0.3 mm.
  • Parts that slide together: 0.2-0.4 mm clearance per side.
  • Connector cut-outs: size for the plug, not the socket, and add 0.5 mm. A USB-C socket is small; the moulded body of the cable that plugs into it is much bigger. A case sized to the socket works with one particular cable and nothing else.

The exact figures depend on your printer, which is why the test plate matters more than any rule.

Clearance, above and below

Measure the tallest thing on each side of the board. Through-hole leads stick out underneath. Capacitors, headers and connectors stand up on top. Leave at least 3 mm between any component and a wall, and more above anything that gets hot or has a fan.

Heat-set inserts, not screws into plastic

A screw driven straight into a printed hole cuts its own thread, and after about ten openings that thread is gone. A brass heat-set insert, pressed in with a soldering iron, gives a real metal thread that survives hundreds.

  • Model the hole about 0.2 mm smaller than the insert's outer diameter.
  • Iron at roughly 220 °C for PETG, a little lower for PLA.
  • Rest the insert on the hole and let the iron's own weight sink it. Do not push - forcing it tips it, and a crooked insert means a screw that never goes in straight.
  • A dedicated insert tip for your iron costs a few dollars and makes this far easier.

Orientation decides strength

A print is strong along its layers and weak between them - the layers can peel apart. So:

  • Do not load screw posts across their layer lines, or they snap off at the base.
  • Make clips and snap-fits flex along the layers, not across them.
  • A thin wall printed flat is stronger than the same wall printed standing up.

Airflow is a path, not a number of holes

Anything that gets warm needs air to move through the case on a deliberate route. Scatter vents everywhere and a fan simply pulls air through the nearest hole, short-circuiting the path.

  • In low, out high. Hot air rises; work with it.
  • Across the hot thing, not around it. Place the inlet and outlet so the only path goes past the heatsink.
  • Inlet area at least as large as the fan. A fan starved by small holes moves a fraction of its rated air and gets loud doing it.
  • Exhaust nowhere near intake, or the case breathes its own exhaust.

For a board that draws a few watts, slots top and bottom and natural convection are enough. For a Jetson at twenty-five, you need a fan and a planned path - see the Jetson case, which also controls that fan from an independent Nano.

Outdoors and wet

A 3D print is not waterproof by default. The gaps between layers are tiny channels, and water finds them - a printed box left in rain will have water inside within days, even with every seam sealed.

  • ASA rather than PLA or PETG for anything in sunlight. It is UV-stable; the others go brittle and chalky.
  • More perimeters - four or five walls instead of two - closes most of the layer channels.
  • A TPU gasket in a groove around the lid, printed in flexible filament, seals the joint the way a rubber O-ring would.
  • Cable entries from below, with a drip loop. A cable entering from the top is a funnel.
  • A small vent on the underside. A perfectly sealed box heats in the sun, cools at night, and pulls moist air in as it contracts - sealed boxes end up wetter inside than vented ones.

For anything that genuinely has to be submerged, print a mould and pot the electronics in epoxy instead, as the gillnet nodes are.

If you are choosing a printer

Any current mid-range printer will print everything on this page. What matters for electronics work:

  • An enclosure, if you want ASA or other materials that warp in a draught. PLA and PETG print fine without one.
  • A hardened nozzle only if you plan to print carbon-fibre or glow-in-the-dark filaments, which wear a brass nozzle out quickly.
  • Reliability over speed. A case that takes three hours and fits beats one that takes one hour and does not.

Designs are freely shared on Printables and Thingiverse, and many parts for the boards here already exist. Check them against your own board with the calipers before trusting them - a model measured from someone else's clone may be a millimetre out for yours.

Printer safety

Do not leave a printer running unattended

A printer runs a hot end at around 250 °C and a heated bed drawing ten amps or more, for hours. Printers occasionally catch fire - most often at the heated bed's power connector, where a loose or badly crimped joint heats, darkens and burns.

  • Check thermal runaway protection is enabled in your printer's firmware. Some older and cheaper printers ship with it off.
  • Inspect the bed connector periodically for darkening, and replace it at the first sign of heat.
  • A certified smoke alarm in the room, and a suitable extinguisher within reach.
  • Do not print overnight or while out until you trust the machine - and the fire guard is an extra layer, not a replacement for any of these.
Fumes and particles

All filaments release ultrafine particles while printing. PLA is the mildest. ABS and ASA also release styrene, which you can smell and should not be breathing for hours in a closed room.

Print in a ventilated room, and enclose and vent the printer if you print ABS or ASA regularly. Do not print in a bedroom.

Burns and heat-set inserts

The hot end, the bed and a freshly printed part are all hot enough to burn. When setting inserts, the insert and the plastic around it stay hot for a while after the iron comes away.
