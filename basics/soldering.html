How to solder

Soldering is not a talent. It is four physical facts and about twenty minutes of practice. Most people who think they are bad at it were given a bad iron and told to "heat the wire and touch the solder to it", which is almost the right instruction and produces almost entirely bad joints.

Reading time20 min
Practice30 joints
Kit needed~$75

What soldering actually is

Solder is a metal alloy that melts at around 190 °C - low enough that a hand tool can melt it, high enough that it stays solid in a warm room. When you melt it onto two clean, hot metal surfaces, it does not simply glue them: it forms a thin intermetallic layer with each, chemically bonding to both. That bond is the joint. Everything else on this page is in service of getting that bond to form.

Three conditions have to be true at the same moment:

  • Both metals must be hot enough. Not the solder - the metals. If you melt solder on the iron and wipe it onto a cold pad, it sticks the way candle wax sticks: mechanically, weakly, and it will fail. This is a cold joint, and it is the single most common fault.
  • Both metals must be chemically clean. Copper grows an oxide skin within hours of being exposed to air, and solder will not bond to oxide. That is what flux is for - it dissolves the oxide at soldering temperature and shields the surface while the joint forms.
  • The solder must be allowed to flow. Molten solder runs towards heat and wets clean metal by capillary action. Your job is to heat the right things and then get out of the way, not to push solder around like paint.
The one sentence version

Heat the joint - not the solder - feed solder into the joint until it flows, take the solder away, then take the iron away, then do not move anything for two seconds.

The kit, and what actually matters

The iron: buy temperature control or do not bother

A fixed-temperature £8 pencil iron has no thermostat. It heats until the heat going in equals the heat leaking out, which might be 250 °C in still air and 480 °C on a sunny windowsill, and which collapses the moment you touch a piece of metal that can absorb heat. That collapse is why beginners with cheap irons make cold joints and conclude they are clumsy.

A temperature-controlled iron measures the tip and pushes power in to hold a set number. Yours does not need to be expensive:

The tip: chisel, not cone

New irons ship with a fine conical tip, which is the worst possible choice for a beginner. A cone touches the work at a single point and moves almost no heat. Use a 2.4 mm chisel (often labelled D24 or 2.4D) for practically everything on this site: headers, wires, through-hole parts. Its flat face makes contact with both the pad and the pin at once.

Keep a 1 mm chisel for tight work. You will not need a needle point unless you get into surface-mount.

The solder

0.8 mm rosin-core is the right diameter for everything here - 1 mm is clumsy on small pads, 0.5 mm means feeding it forever. "Rosin core" means the flux is already inside the wire as a hollow channel, which is why you rarely need a separate flux pen for through-hole work.

Leaded 60/40 (60 % tin, 40 % lead) melts at 183-190 °C, flows beautifully and is much easier to learn on. Lead-free (SAC305) melts around 217-220 °C, is stiffer, looks duller when correct, and is what you should use if children or pregnancy are anywhere in the picture. Either way: do not eat it, wash your hands afterwards, and open a window.

Setting up, before you melt anything

  1. Sit down properly

    Elbows supported on the desk. A soldering iron held by an unsupported arm wobbles by a couple of millimetres, which on a 2.54 mm pitch header is the difference between a joint and a bridge. Rest your forearms and move from the wrist and fingers.

  2. Set the temperature

    340 °C for leaded solder, 370 °C for lead-free. Those are starting points, not laws.

    The instinct when a joint will not take is to turn the iron down "to be safe". Do the opposite. A hotter iron finishes the joint in two seconds and puts less total heat into the part than a cool iron struggling for ten. Ten seconds at 300 °C will lift a pad off a cheap board; three seconds at 370 °C will not.

  3. Tin the tip, always

    As soon as the iron reaches temperature, melt a little solder onto the flat face until it is bright and silver. That coating is what carries heat from the tip into the work; a dry, dull, oxidised tip transfers almost nothing and is the second most common reason a beginner "cannot solder".

    Re-tin between joints. Wipe in brass wool, add a touch of fresh solder, go. And always leave a blob of solder on the tip when you switch off - it protects the tip overnight.

  4. Hold the iron like a pen

    Not like a knife, not like a screwdriver. Pen grip, close to the ferrule, hand braced against the bench.

  5. Put the iron in the stand every single time

    Not on the desk "just for a second". This is the rule that stops the burn. Make it automatic before you make anything else automatic.

The three-second joint

This is the core motion. Everything else on this page is a variation of it. Practise it on a scrap perfboard with cut resistor legs about thirty times before you touch a project.

  1. Put the parts together first

    Component leg through the hole, header in place, wire laid on the pad. Solder does not hold things in position while you work - gravity, tape or a vice does.

  2. Touch the tip to BOTH metals at once

    Lay the flat face of the chisel so it bridges the pad and the pin. This is the single most important detail on the page. Touching only the pin heats the pin; the pad stays cold; the solder makes a shiny blob around the pin that is attached to nothing.

    Count one.

  3. Feed solder into the joint, not onto the iron

    Touch the solder wire to the far side of the joint, where the pad meets the pin - the opposite side from the iron. If the metals are hot enough it will melt on contact and run towards the tip, flooding the gap. That flow is the sign the joint is working.

    If you have to touch the solder to the iron to melt it, the joint is not hot enough. Stop, wait another second, try again.

    Count two. Feed roughly 2-3 mm of 0.8 mm solder for a header pin.

  4. Solder away first, then the iron

    Pull the solder wire back. Leave the iron for about half a second more so the flux finishes working and the surface smooths out. Then lift the iron cleanly, straight up.

    Count three. The whole thing took three seconds.

  5. Do not move it for two seconds

    Solder stays liquid for a moment after the iron leaves. Move the part now and it freezes with a crystalline, crumbly structure - a disturbed joint, dull and grainy and mechanically weak. Just wait. Breathe out.

  6. Look at it

    A good joint is a smooth concave fillet - a little volcano hugging the pin and spreading onto the pad, shiny with leaded solder, satin with lead-free. You should still be able to see the shape of the pin inside it.

The tug test

When it has cooled, pull the component gently. A good joint does not move at all. Do this on every joint while you are learning - it takes a second and it teaches your hands what "right" felt like.

Pin headers: the job you will do most

Nearly every module you buy arrives with a loose strip of pin header in the bag. Soldering it on is the first real soldering most people do, and it has one trick that makes it easy.

  1. Break the strip to length

    Headers snap on the 2.54 mm grid. Count the pins you need, then cut with flush cutters through the plastic between the pin you want and the one you do not - you will sacrifice one pin. Some people score and snap with pliers; cutters are cleaner.

  2. Get the orientation right - this is the part people get wrong

    The long side of the pins goes through the board; the plastic spacer sits flat on the component side; the short stubs stick out the back where you solder. For a module that will plug into a breadboard, the pins point down, away from the components. Hold the module the way it will be used and check twice. Unsoldering eight pins to flip a header is an unpleasant half hour.

  3. Use the breadboard as a jig

    Push the header into a breadboard, pins down. Drop the module onto the protruding pins. The breadboard now holds everything square and at the right height, for free. This is the trick.

  4. Tack the two end pins only

    Solder pin 1 and the last pin. Then look from the side: is the plastic flat against the board, is the module square? If not, reheat one tack joint and nudge it straight. Two joints are easy to redo. Ten are not.

  5. Then run the rest

    Three seconds each, working along the row. Wipe and re-tin the tip every four or five joints.

    If you are doing a long row, alternate ends rather than going straight along - it spreads the heat and stops the plastic softening and sagging.

  6. Inspect along the row at a low angle

    Hold the board up so light rakes across the joints. Bridges and cold balls are obvious from the side and nearly invisible from directly above.

Melted header plastic

If the black plastic starts to soften and the pins go crooked, you are spending too long on each pin. Turn the iron up by 20 °C and move faster. Counter-intuitive, completely true.

Wires: splices, pads and heat-shrink

Tin the wire first

Strip 4-5 mm. Twist the strands tight between finger and thumb in the direction they are already laid. Hold the iron under the bare strands and feed solder in from above until the bundle turns silver and goes stiff. That is tinning, and it does three jobs: it stops strands splaying, it removes the oxide, and it means the actual joint takes one second instead of five.

Do not tin a wire that goes into a screw terminal

Solder creeps under pressure. A tinned wire clamped in a screw terminal slowly flattens, the screw loosens, and months later you have an intermittent connection or a hot spot. Bare twisted strands, or a crimped ferrule, for anything that gets clamped.

Joining two wires (the lineman's splice)

  1. Heat-shrink on first

    Cut 20 mm of tubing and slide it onto one wire, well away from where you will be working. Every person who has ever soldered has finished a perfect joint and then found the heat-shrink still sitting on the bench. Do it first, every time.

  2. Strip 8 mm on each and tin both

    Longer than you think, because the splice uses it up.

  3. Cross them and wrap

    Lay the two tinned ends across each other in an X, then twist each end around the opposite wire two or three turns. You want a joint that holds together mechanically before any solder is added. A straight butt joint held only by solder will snap.

  4. Heat and feed

    Iron underneath the splice, solder fed from on top. The solder should wick through the whole twist and leave it looking like one piece of silver wire, not a lump. Two or three seconds.

  5. Slide the tubing over and shrink it

    A hot-air gun, a lighter held well below the flame, or the side of the soldering iron barrel rolled against it. Never the tip.

Soldering a wire to a pad or a hole

Tin the wire. Tin the pad - touch the iron to the pad and feed a small amount of solder onto it, then remove both. Now hold the tinned wire against the tinned pad and touch the iron to the joint for one second: the two pools merge instantly. This two-step method is much easier than trying to hold a wire, an iron and a solder reel against a pad at the same moment.

Undoing it

Every build involves at least one thing going in backwards. Desoldering is a normal part of the craft, not an admission of failure.

A bridge between two pins

The easiest fix there is. Wipe the tip clean, add a tiny amount of fresh solder to it (the flux in it is what does the work), then drag the flat of the tip across the bridge perpendicular to the pins and lift away. Surface tension pulls the excess onto the tip. If it will not go, add flux and try again.

Getting solder out of a hole

Lay desoldering braid over the joint, press the iron down on top of the braid, and wait. You will see the braid darken as it fills - that is the solder wicking up into it by capillary action. Lift the braid and the iron together; if you let the braid cool onto the pad you will glue it there and tear the pad off when you pull. Snip off the used section and repeat with fresh braid.

A solder sucker is faster on through-holes: heat the joint until molten, bring the nozzle down over it, hit the trigger. Braid is better for flat pads and for cleaning up afterwards.

Removing a multi-pin part

This is the genuinely hard case, because all the pins must be liquid at once and they are not. Options, in order of preference:

  • Cut the part off first. If you do not need to keep it, snip each leg with flush cutters, remove the body, then remove the legs one at a time. Twenty times easier.
  • Add more solder. Counter-intuitive but effective: flooding all the pins with a big blob of fresh leaded solder keeps them molten together long enough to pull the part.
  • Braid every pin, then work the part loose while gently heating each hole in turn.
If you lift a pad

The copper ring comes away from the board and the connection is gone. Do not throw the board out. Scrape 1 mm of green solder mask off the trace that led to that pad with a craft knife until bare copper shows, tin it, and solder a thin wire from there to the component leg. It is ugly and it works perfectly.

Not burning yourself or the house

  • The tip is at 340 °C and looks exactly the same as it does at room temperature. Assume it is always hot.
  • If you burn yourself: cold running water for ten minutes. Not ice, not butter, not a plaster immediately. Most soldering burns are small and shallow; a burn that blisters badly or is larger than a coin should be seen by a doctor.
  • Never catch a falling iron. Let it fall and step back. This is worth rehearsing in your head, because the reflex to grab is strong.
  • Do not solder anything connected to mains power, or anything plugged into a wall, ever. Unplug it, and if it has big capacitors in it, wait.
  • Ventilate. Flux smoke is a respiratory irritant, and repeated exposure sensitises some people permanently.
  • Wash your hands before eating, especially with leaded solder. That is genuinely the whole of the lead precaution for hand soldering - the fumes are flux, not lead, because lead does not vaporise at these temperatures.
  • Keep the solder reel away from the tip. A reel that touches a hot iron unrolls itself into a hot mess.

A half hour of practice that is worth a year of guessing

Buy two 5×7 cm perfboards and a bag of resistors you do not care about. Then:

  1. Thirty solo joints

    Push resistor legs through random holes and solder each one. Do not rush. After each, look at it and name it: good, cold, disturbed, too much. Naming them is what builds the eye.

  2. Ten joints upside down

    Turn the board so you are working towards yourself, or at an awkward angle. Real projects are never conveniently oriented.

  3. A full 8-pin header

    Using the breadboard jig. Time yourself. Under a minute for eight joints is a perfectly normal target once it clicks.

  4. Deliberately make three bridges, then clear them

    Getting comfortable with fixing mistakes removes most of the fear, and the fear is what makes people hover and overheat things.

  5. Deliberately make a cold joint

    Touch the solder to the iron and wipe it onto a cold pad. Look at it closely - the convex ball with the sharp edge where it meets the copper. Now you will recognise it instantly for the rest of your life.
