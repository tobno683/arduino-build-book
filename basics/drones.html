Building a drone

The background the drone projects assume you already have. What the classes are, how to size a power system so the numbers agree with each other, and the specific mistakes that turn a $300 build into a box of broken parts.

Start here, whatever you eventually want

Learn to fly before you learn to build

Buy a simulator - Liftoff, Velocidrone or DRL, about $20 - and a decent radio. Spend ten hours in it before you order a single part.

This is not optional advice and it is not gatekeeping. Ten hours of simulator costs nothing to crash and will save you several hundred dollars of real parts. Every experienced pilot will tell you the same thing and almost every beginner ignores it.

Then get a cheap ready-to-fly whoop and learn indoors. Only after that does building your own make sense, because you will know what you are trying to build and you will be able to tell a flying problem from a build problem.

The classes, and what each is for

Almost every decision downstream follows from this one. A 7 inch long-range build and a 5 inch racer share almost no parts and almost no design thinking.

Thrust to weight: the number everything else serves

Total thrust from all four motors at full throttle, divided by the all-up weight of the finished quad with its battery. Every other choice in the power system exists to hit a target here.

Work backwards from it. Suppose you want a 5 inch freestyle quad at 5:1:

  1. Estimate all-up weight. A 5 inch build is around 650 g with a 6S 1300 mAh pack.
  2. Required total thrust = 650 g × 5 = 3,250 g.
  3. Per motor = 3,250 ÷ 4 = 813 g.
  4. Find a motor and prop combination that produces at least that. Every reputable motor manufacturer publishes thrust tables per prop and per cell count - use them rather than guessing.
  5. Read the current off the same row. If each motor pulls 30 A at full throttle, the quad pulls 120 A, and now you know what the ESC and the battery must survive.

That last step is the one people skip, and it is where builds go wrong. The thrust number picks the motor; the current number picks the ESC and the battery.

Motors: stator size and KV

A brushless motor is described by two numbers, and both matter more than the brand.

Stator size

A motor called 2207 has a stator 22 mm across and 7 mm tall. Bigger means more torque, which means it can swing a bigger or more aggressive prop without overheating. It also means more weight and more rotating inertia, so it responds slightly more slowly.

  • Width (the first two digits) buys torque efficiently.
  • Height (the second two) buys torque at more cost in weight and responsiveness.

Match it to the prop. A 5 inch prop wants a 2207 class motor; a 7 inch wants a 2806 or 2807; a 3 inch wants a 1404 to 1507. Putting a big prop on a small motor is the most common cause of motors you cannot hold.

KV

KV is revolutions per minute per volt, unloaded. A 1900 KV motor on a fully charged 6S pack (25.2 V) spins at about 47,000 rpm with nothing attached. With a prop on it, far less - but the number still tells you what you need to know relative to other motors.

The rule that matters: KV and cell count trade against each other. Going from 4S to 6S is a 50% voltage increase, so drop KV by about a third to keep the same rpm:

  • 4S at 2400 KV ≈ 6S at 1600 KV
  • 4S at 2600 KV ≈ 6S at 1750 KV

Fit a 4S-rated high-KV motor to a 6S pack and it will spin far too fast for the prop, draw enormous current, and cook itself within a couple of flights.

Why 6S has largely won

The same power at higher voltage means lower current. Lower current means thinner wire, cooler ESCs, less loss in the connectors and less voltage sag. A 6S build at the same power is measurably more efficient than the 4S equivalent, and the parts are no more expensive.

4S is still fine, and it is what most sub-250 g and 3 inch builds use.

Propellers: diameter, pitch, blades

A prop marked 5143 is 5.1 inches across with a 4.3 inch pitch. Pitch is the distance it would advance in one rotation through a solid, which is a useful fiction - it tells you how aggressive the blade angle is.

Diameter

More diameter moves more air, so more thrust and more efficiency - but it needs more torque, so a bigger motor, and it accelerates and decelerates more slowly.

Diameter is the main lever for efficiency. It is why long-range builds are 7 inch and racers are not.

Pitch

More pitch is a steeper blade: higher top speed, much more current, and less thrust at low throttle because the blade stalls.

If your motors run hot, reducing pitch is usually the first thing to try.

Blade count

Two blades are the most efficient. Three give more thrust and much better grip in hard manoeuvres for noticeably more current. Four and five are for ducted builds and pure thrust.

Tri-blade is the freestyle default; bi-blade is for racing and range.

The practical approach

Props are a dollar each. Buy three or four combinations, fly the same pack on each, and land after one minute to feel the motors. The one that gives the flight you want without hot motors is the right one.

This is cheaper and more reliable than any amount of calculation.

Batteries: cells, capacity and the C rating lie

A LiPo is described as, say, 6S 1300 mAh 100C. Three numbers, and one of them is fiction.

  • S is cells in series. Each cell is 3.7 V nominal, 4.2 V fully charged and 3.0 V damaged. A 6S pack is 22.2 V nominal and 25.2 V charged.
  • mAh is capacity. More capacity means more flight time and more weight, and the two fight each other - past a point the extra weight eats the extra energy. For a 5 inch build, 1300 mAh on 6S is the sweet spot; for a 7 inch, 3000-5000 mAh.
  • C is the claimed continuous discharge. Capacity × C = amps. A 1300 mAh 100C pack claims 130 A.
Halve every C rating you read

C ratings are marketing numbers and have been for twenty years. A pack sold as 100C will typically deliver something like 50C continuously without sagging badly or getting hot.

Size for half the printed number, and judge packs by how much they sag under load rather than by what is on the label.

Voltage under load is the real measurement. A healthy pack sags perhaps 0.3 V per cell at full throttle and recovers immediately. A tired one sags a volt and stays down. Judge by resting voltage after landing - if it recovers above 3.7 V per cell you landed earlier than you needed to, and below 3.5 V you are shortening the pack's life.

ESCs, flight controllers and the radio link

ESCs

Size by the current you calculated from the thrust table, with headroom. If the quad pulls 120 A at full throttle, a 4-in-1 rated 45 A per motor is comfortable and a 35 A one is not.

  • 4-in-1 is the default. One board, one set of power wires, far less soldering.
  • Firmware: BLHeli_32 was the standard and is now effectively replaced by AM32, which is open source and actively developed. BLHeli_S boards can usually run Bluejay.
  • Protocol: DShot600 for 5 inch, DShot300 for larger. Use bidirectional DShot if the firmware supports it - it feeds real motor rpm back to the flight controller and enables RPM filtering, which is the single biggest tuning improvement of the last decade.

Flight controllers

F4 is cheap and adequate, F7 gives headroom, H7 is for running everything at once. For most builds the processor is not the limit.

What matters more:

  • The gyro. ICM-42688P is the current standard and is excellent. The old MPU6000 is slower but famously smooth and still fine.
  • Soft mounting. Rubber grommets in the stack. This affects flight quality more than the choice of chip does.
  • UART count. Receiver, video system, GPS, telemetry - you run out faster than you expect.

Betaflight for freestyle and racing, INAV for GPS-heavy and fixed-wing, ArduPilot for autonomous work.

The radio link

ExpressLRS at 2.4 GHz or 915/868 MHz is the answer for new builds - open source, extremely long range, low latency, and cheap. TBS Crossfire is the established commercial alternative.

Whatever you use, configure and test the failsafe. See the pitfalls below.

Video

  • Analogue - cheap, universally compatible, degrades gracefully as it gets weak. Still entirely viable and it is what most people learn on.
  • Digital - DJI, Walksnail and HDZero. Enormously better image, more expensive, heavier, and it can cut out abruptly where analogue would just get noisy.

Frame geometry

The frame decides how it flies and how it survives crashing. Arm thickness matters most - 5 mm carbon for a 5 inch build, 4 mm if you are chasing weight and are prepared to break arms.

The pitfalls, specifically

These are the things that actually ruin builds. Most of them cost nothing to avoid and a lot to fix.

Safety

Propellers cut. This is the whole safety section in one line.

A 5 inch tri-blade at 30,000 rpm will go through to the bone. This is not a theoretical risk - it is the most common serious injury in the hobby and it nearly always happens on a bench, not in flight.

  • Props off for every bench test. Every single one, including the one where you are certain nothing can go wrong. Motor direction checks, ESC configuration, receiver binding - all props off.
  • Props on last, outdoors, with the quad pointing away from you and everybody else.
  • Disarm before you approach it. Then put the transmitter down before you pick the quad up, so you cannot arm it with your knee.
  • Eye protection when working on a powered quad. Props shed blades.
  • Never fly over people. Not low, not high, not "just past them".
Lithium polymer batteries

Consistent with every other lithium project on this site, plus the things specific to high-discharge packs:

  • Charge in a bag or an ammo tin, on a non-flammable surface, never unattended and never overnight.
  • Never charge a puffed, damaged or crashed pack. A hard crash can cause internal damage that shows up hours later - set a crashed pack aside somewhere safe and watch it.
  • Never charge below 0 °C. It plates lithium onto the anode permanently and can cause an internal short later.
  • Storage charge - about 3.8 V per cell - for anything not being flown within a day or two. Leaving packs fully charged is the fastest way to kill them.
  • Do not discharge below 3.5 V per cell under load, or 3.0 V ever.
  • A LiPo fire cannot be put out with water. It supplies its own oxidiser. Sand, or let it burn somewhere it cannot set anything else alight.
Use a smoke stopper for the first power-up

A mains lamp or a car bulb in series with the battery lead. A solder bridge on the ESC pads - the most common build fault there is - then makes the bulb glow instead of destroying the stack and possibly starting a fire.

It costs about five dollars to make and it pays for itself the first time.

The rules, in outline

Drone regulation is national, changes regularly, and is enforced. What follows is the shape of it in Europe and the UK as of late 2026 - check your own authority before flying, because the detail differs and this page will go stale.

  • Operator registration. Required for anything over 250 g, and for any drone with a camera regardless of weight - which is every FPV quad. You get an operator number that goes on the aircraft.
  • Class marks. Drones sold in the EU carry a C0 to C6 label setting where they may be flown. Self-built aircraft are exempt from class marking, which is a genuine advantage of building your own, though the other rules still apply.
  • Remote ID. Required for C1, C2 and C3 class drones - broadcasting operator ID and position over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth. C0, under 250 g, is exempt in the Open category, which is a real reason to consider a sub-250 g build.
  • Visual line of sight. The default requirement almost everywhere. FPV flying is generally permitted only with a competent spotter beside you maintaining that line of sight.
  • Where you cannot fly. Airports and their approaches, over crowds, and a long list of restricted areas. Use your national airspace app - they are free and they are the authoritative answer.
  • Insurance. Required in some countries, sensible everywhere. National model flying associations usually include it in membership and it is cheap.
Sub-250 g is worth considering seriously

Staying under 250 g all-up sidesteps a meaningful amount of regulation in most jurisdictions, and modern 3 inch and toothpick builds are genuinely capable. If you are choosing a first build and the rules where you live are strict, this is a real option rather than a compromise.
