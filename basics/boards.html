Which board

Thirteen boards, what each one is genuinely for, and the specific ways each will waste an evening of your time. Most projects do not need a fast board - they need the right one, and those are different questions.

If you have never bought one

That is the whole answer. The rest of this page is for when you have hit a limit and want to know which board removes it.

Buy two of whatever you buy

The second board is not a spare. It is a diagnostic tool: when something does not work, swapping the board tells you in thirty seconds whether the fault is your wiring or your hardware. At these prices that is the cheapest test equipment you will ever own.

The one thing that destroys boards

5 volt and 3.3 volt logic are not interchangeable, and mixing them is how most dead boards die. It is worth understanding once, properly, because it applies to everything on this page.

The classic Arduinos - Uno, Nano, Mega, Pro Micro - run at 5 V. Everything modern - ESP32, ESP8266, Pico, Nano 33, and all three Linux boards - runs at 3.3 V. Two consequences follow, and only one of them is obvious:

  • A 5 V output into a 3.3 V input can destroy it. This is the dangerous direction. Connecting an Uno's TX pin straight to an ESP32's RX pin is the textbook example, and it sometimes works for weeks before failing.
  • A 3.3 V output into a 5 V input often just does not work, because 3.3 V may not be high enough to read as a logical 1. This direction is usually harmless but confusing - the wire is connected and nothing happens.

The fix is a level shifter, which is a dollar. A resistor divider works for one direction. And a few 5 V parts are happy being driven at 3.3 V - WS2812 LEDs usually are, at the edge of their specification.

The Nano 33 BLE Sense trap

It has a Nano's shape, a Nano's pin labels and a Nano's name, and it is 3.3 V and not 5 V tolerant. Dropping one into an existing Uno-based project as an upgrade is an expensive way to learn this. Same for the Jetson's 40-pin header.

Clones, and what you actually lose

An official Uno is around $28 and a clone is around $5. The chip is the same, the sketch is the same, and the clone is not a counterfeit - the designs are open hardware and making them is the point.

What differs:

  • The USB chip. Official boards use an ATmega16U2; clones use a CH340 or CP2102. Both work, but the CH340 needs a driver on Windows and older macOS, and the board looks completely dead until you install it. This is the single most common "my new board does not work".
  • The voltage regulator. Clones often fit a smaller one. Fine at normal loads, and it will get hot if you draw 500 mA through the 5 V pin.
  • Headers. Usually supplied loose. Soldering them is the perfect first soldering job - see the soldering guide.
  • Quality control. Occasionally a bad board. At a fifth of the price, buying two is still cheaper.

Buy clones. If you want to support the Arduino project, buy one official board once - that is a reasonable thing to do, and it is a donation rather than a purchase.

How you know you have outgrown a board

There are only about five real limits, and each has an obvious next step.

You ran out of RAM

The symptom is not a compiler error. It is a sketch that compiles, runs for a while, then behaves insanely - garbled output, random resets, a variable that changes on its own. That is the stack and the heap colliding.

Next: an ESP32 has 150 times the RAM for less money. Or fix it in place by moving constant strings to flash with F() and PROGMEM, which often buys back hundreds of bytes.

You ran out of pins

Counting the wires and coming up short.

Next: usually not a bigger board. A PCF8574 gives eight more I/O over two I2C wires for under a dollar; a 74HC595 gives eight outputs for three pins; a CD4051 reads eight analog inputs on one. A Mega is the last resort, not the first.

It is too slow

Rarer than people think. If you are reading a sensor and blinking something, 16 MHz is not your problem. It becomes real with audio, FFTs, addressable LED strips over a few hundred pixels, or anything using floating point in a loop.

Next: an ESP32 or a Pico. Both are 32-bit with hardware floating point, which is the part that matters - not the clock number.

It needs to talk to something

Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, a phone, an API.

Next: an ESP32, and it costs less than the Nano you are replacing. Bolting a radio module onto an Uno costs more and works worse.

It needs to see or listen

Cameras and real audio processing are where microcontrollers genuinely stop.

Next: an ESP32-CAM for cheap video, a UNO Q for vision with Python libraries, a Jetson when a model will not run anywhere else.

It has to last on a battery

A Nano running flat out is about a week on AAs. Most of that is the power LED and the regulator, not the chip.

Next: often the same board, used properly - deep sleep, the LED removed, the regulator bypassed. Months instead of days, for free. An ESP32 sleeps at 10 uA and wakes on a pin.

Good boards this site does not use

Not because they are bad - because every project here has to name one board, and covering fewer means covering them properly.

  • Seeed XIAO family - thumbnail-sized boards in SAMD21, RP2040, ESP32-C3 and nRF52840 flavours. Excellent when size is the constraint, and the newer ones have lithium charging built in.
  • Raspberry Pi 5 and Zero 2 W - full Linux computers. A Zero 2 W is a genuinely good answer for camera projects, and the Pi ecosystem is enormous. The UNO Q covers similar ground here with a microcontroller attached.
  • STM32 "Blue Pill" - very cheap 32-bit with excellent peripherals, and a steeper start because the tooling assumes you know what you are doing.
  • Teensy 4.x - a 600 MHz Cortex-M7, by a distance the fastest microcontroller board you can buy, and the standard answer for serious audio work.
  • ESP32-S3 and C6 - newer ESP32 variants with native USB, more AI-friendly instructions, and on the C6, Thread and Zigbee. Everything on this site that runs on an ESP32 runs on an S3.

New boards worth knowing about are on the news shelf, which says which of them change a project here and which change nothing.
