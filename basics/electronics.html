Enough electronics

Not a course. This is the specific set of facts that, if you do not know them, will cost you a dead board, a burnt sensor or an evening of confusion. Everything here comes up in the projects.

Volts, amps, ohms, watts

The plumbing analogy is imperfect but it gets you a long way:

QuantityPlumbingWhat it means here
Voltage (V)PressureThe push. A 5 V supply pushes twice as hard as 2.5 V. Measured between two points - "5 volts" always means 5 volts relative to something, normally ground.
Current (A)Flow rateHow much is actually moving. A device draws current; a supply does not push it in. This is the single most useful correction to most people's mental model.
Resistance (Ω)Pipe narrownessHow hard it is to get current through.
Power (W)Total energy per secondVolts × amps. What turns into heat.

Ohm's law is the whole of it: V = I × R. Rearranged, I = V / R and R = V / I. Almost every calculation in this hobby is one of those three.

The thing that trips everybody

A 5 V 2 A supply does not force 2 A into your Arduino. It offers up to 2 A at 5 V. An Arduino that wants 50 mA takes 50 mA. Buying a bigger supply never hurts the device; buying too small a one makes the voltage sag, and sagging voltage is what causes mysterious resets.

Why an LED needs a resistor

An LED is not a resistor. It has a forward voltage - a threshold below which almost no current flows, and above which current rises almost vertically. Connect a red LED (about 2 V forward) straight across 5 V and Ohm's law says the current is limited only by the LED's own tiny internal resistance and the wiring. It will briefly be very bright, then be nothing.

The series resistor takes up the difference. With a 5 V supply and a 2 V LED, the resistor has 3 V across it, and you choose its value to set the current:

R = (Vsupply − VLED) / Idesired

Resistor calculator

Any resistor at or above the calculated value is safe; higher just means dimmer. In practice 220 Ω is the default for a 5 V indicator LED and 330 Ω or 1 kΩ if it is annoyingly bright. Modern LEDs are perfectly visible at 2 mA; the traditional 20 mA figure comes from an era of dim parts.

The long leg is the anode (+), the short leg the cathode (−), and there is a flat spot on the rim on the cathode side for when the legs have been trimmed. The resistor can go on either side - it limits the current in the loop and the loop does not care where the restriction is.

Practical consequences:

  • One LED per pin is fine. A motor is not. Anything with a coil or a heater needs a transistor, a MOSFET or a relay, driven by the pin, powered from elsewhere.
  • A servo is not fine either, despite everybody doing it. An SG90 stalls at about 700 mA. It works on the 5 V pin right up until it does not, and the symptom is the board resetting whenever the servo moves.
  • The Uno's 3.3 V pin is almost useless for powering things. An ESP-01 wants 300 mA in bursts. It will brown out. Use a separate regulator.
  • A WS2812 LED at full white draws 60 mA. Thirty of them is 1.8 A. Do the sum before you plug the strip into the board.

Pull-ups, pull-downs, and floating pins

An input pin that is not connected to anything does not read LOW. It reads whatever stray electromagnetic noise is nearby - your hand, the mains wiring in the wall, the motor next to it. That is a floating pin, and it produces the classic beginner symptom: a button that seems to press itself.

A button connects two things. When it is open, one side of it is connected to nothing. So you add a resistor that gently holds the pin at a known level when the button is not doing anything:

  • Pull-up: resistor from the pin to 5 V, button from the pin to ground. Idle reads HIGH; pressed reads LOW.
  • Pull-down: resistor from the pin to ground, button from the pin to 5 V. Idle reads LOW; pressed reads HIGH.

10 kΩ is the standard value: strong enough to beat the noise, weak enough that pressing the button does not waste much current.

You usually do not need the resistor at all

Every Arduino pin has a pull-up resistor built into the chip. pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP) switches it on. Then you wire the button from pin 2 straight to ground, with no resistor anywhere, and read a pressed button as LOW. Every button in this book is wired that way.

I2C is the other place pull-ups appear: the bus needs one resistor from SDA to 3.3/5 V and one from SCL. Almost every breakout module has a pair fitted already, which is why you can usually ignore this - until you chain six modules together and the accumulated pull-ups get too strong and the bus stops working.

Voltage dividers, and reading things safely

Two resistors in series across a voltage; the point between them sits at a predictable fraction:

Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Uses you will actually hit:

  • Measuring a battery. A 12 V battery into an analog pin destroys it. Two 100 kΩ resistors halve it to 6 V - still too much for a 5 V board, so use 100 kΩ and 33 kΩ to get 3 V, and multiply back up in software.
  • Reading a sensor that outputs 5 V into a 3.3 V ESP32. 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ gives 3.3 V. Fine for slow signals; too slow for SPI.
  • Any resistive sensor - LDR, thermistor, potentiometer - is one half of a divider, and the fixed resistor you add is the other half.
A divider is not a level shifter

It works for a signal going into a lower-voltage chip, slowly. It does not work in the other direction, it does not work at SPI speeds, and it cannot supply current. For bidirectional or fast signals use a proper level-shifter module - they cost about a dollar.

3.3 V versus 5 V, and what actually dies

An Uno runs its logic at 5 V. An ESP32, an ESP8266, a Raspberry Pi Pico and most modern sensors run at 3.3 V. Mixing them has two separate questions, and people conflate them constantly:

  1. Power. Many 3.3 V modules have a regulator on the breakout board and a pin marked VIN or VCC that happily takes 5 V. Check for a three-pin regulator and a 3V3 label on the board.
  2. Signals. Separate issue. Feeding a 5 V logic high into a 3.3 V input pin pushes current through its internal protection diodes. Sometimes it survives for years. Sometimes it dies in a minute. The RC522 RFID reader is the famous casualty.

Going the other way is usually free: a 3.3 V high is above the ~3 V an ATmega needs to read a HIGH, so a 3.3 V sensor talking to an Uno generally just works.

Decoupling capacitors, and why things reset randomly

When a chip switches, it grabs a gulp of current in a few nanoseconds. The wire from the regulator has inductance, so it cannot supply that instantly, and the local voltage dips. Dip far enough and the chip resets - or reads garbage, or the display corrupts.

A capacitor placed right next to the chip is a tiny local reservoir that supplies the gulp:

  • 100 nF ceramic across VCC and GND, as close to the chip as physically possible. Handles the fast, small gulps. Modules have these fitted already.
  • 10-100 µF electrolytic near anything that switches hard - motors, relays, Wi-Fi radios. Handles the slower, bigger sags.
  • 470-1000 µF across the supply of an LED strip, right at the input end. The FastLED documentation insists on this and it is right.

The nRF24L01 radio is the notorious example: without a 10 µF capacitor soldered across its VCC and GND it works for thirty seconds and then behaves like a faulty module. Thousands of them have been thrown away for want of a five-cent part.

Motors, relays, and the spike that kills transistors

Anything with a coil in it - a motor, a relay, a solenoid, a buzzer - stores energy in a magnetic field while current flows. Cut the current suddenly and the field collapses, and the coil generates a voltage spike in the opposite direction, which can be hundreds of volts for a microsecond. That spike is what destroys the transistor that just switched off, and what resets your Arduino across the bench.

The fix is a flyback diode: a 1N4007 wired backwards across the coil - cathode (the striped end) to the positive side. In normal operation it does nothing. At the moment of switch-off it gives the collapsing field a harmless loop to dump into.

Relay modules have this diode fitted on the board. A bare relay does not. Motors always want one, and a motor driver board like an L298N has them internally.

And give motors their own supply

Motor current through the same wires that feed your board is the other half of the problem. Separate supplies, with the two grounds tied together - the ground connection is what lets the signals be understood by both. Common ground, separate power. Say it until it is reflex.

Analog in, "analog" out

analogRead() runs a 10-bit converter: it returns 0-1023 where 0 is 0 V and 1023 is the reference voltage (5 V by default on an Uno). So volts = reading * 5.0 / 1023.0. It takes about 100 µs, so roughly 10,000 readings a second is the ceiling. An ESP32 gives 12 bits (0-4095) and its converter is noticeably non-linear near the ends of its range.

analogWrite() does not produce an analog voltage at all. It produces PWM - a square wave switching between 0 V and 5 V at about 490 Hz, where the number 0-255 sets what fraction of each cycle is spent high. An LED averages it and looks dim. A motor averages it and turns slowly. A multimeter averages it and lies to you convincingly. A speaker does not average it, which is why PWM audio sounds harsh.

Only certain pins can do it - on an Uno, the ones marked with a tilde: 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11.

The ESP32 row is why so many ESP32 projects are unreliable on a laptop USB port: the average is modest but the transmit bursts are brutal, and a marginal supply drops out at exactly the moment the radio fires. If an ESP32 project resets whenever it tries to connect to Wi-Fi, that is your answer nine times out of ten.
